AI Act deadlines pushed back: what the Digital Omnibus means for your business

The EU has given companies until December 2027 to meet its strictest AI rules. But some obligations already apply — and most small businesses have homework to do now.

By European SME Editorial Team · 7 November 2026 · 4 min read
Key points
  • Rules for high-risk AI — such as tools used in hiring, credit scoring or education — now apply from 2 December 2027, not August 2026.
  • Transparency duties, such as telling people they are talking to a chatbot, have applied since 2 August 2026.
  • Companies just above the SME thresholds, now called “small mid-caps”, get some of the same relief as SMEs.

What changed

The Digital Omnibus on AI — Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 — was published in the Official Journal on 24 July and entered into force on 27 July 2026, just days before the AI Act's original high-risk deadline. It does not rewrite the AI Act. It moves its timetable.

Obligations for standalone high-risk AI systems listed in Annex III of the Act — used in areas such as recruitment, credit scoring, education and critical infrastructure — now apply from 2 December 2027 instead of 2 August 2026. High-risk AI built into products already covered by EU safety law, such as medical devices or machinery, follows on 2 August 2028.

Why the delay

The reason is practical. Companies prove they comply with the high-risk rules largely by following harmonised technical standards — and those standards are not ready. As of May 2026 none had been formally published; the European standards bodies CEN and CENELEC are still drafting them. The new dates are meant to line up with when guidance and standards become available.

A later deadline is not an exemption. Several parts of the AI Act already apply.

What already applies

The ban on prohibited AI practices and the duty to make sure staff using AI have adequate AI literacy have applied since February 2025. Rules for general-purpose AI models followed in August 2025.

Since 2 August 2026, transparency obligations under Article 50 apply as well. In practice, people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system such as a chatbot, and certain AI-generated content must be labelled as such. For many small businesses, this is the part of the AI Act that matters most today.

Relief for growing companies

The Omnibus introduces a new category of “small mid-cap” enterprises — companies that have outgrown the EU's SME thresholds but are far from large — and extends several of the AI Act's SME measures to them. Until now, crossing the SME line meant losing that lighter treatment overnight.

What to do now

  • List every AI tool your business uses, including AI features inside software you already pay for.
  • Flag anything that screens job applicants, scores customers' creditworthiness or makes decisions about people — these are the likely high-risk uses.
  • Check chatbots and AI-generated marketing content against the transparency rules that already apply.
  • Ask your vendors, in writing, how and when they will meet the high-risk requirements.
