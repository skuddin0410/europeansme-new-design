How to Write a Business Plan That Actually Gets Funded

Investors and lenders read hundreds of plans. The ones that succeed are not the most polished or the most optimistic — they are the most credible.

A business plan is not a formality. It is the document that forces you to confront every assumption you have made about your market, your customers and your competitive position — and to test whether those assumptions hold up when examined honestly.

The purpose most founders miss

Most SME owners write a business plan only when a bank or investor requires one. This gets the causality backwards. The most valuable function of a business plan is not to persuade an external party — it is to stress-test your own thinking before you commit significant time and capital.

Putting a market analysis, a competitive assessment and a financial model on paper reveals gaps that no amount of enthusiasm can paper over. Write it for yourself first.

The version that earns funding is a byproduct of the version that makes you genuinely confident in what you are building.

The sections that actually matter

Every funded business plan contains six substantive elements:

  • An executive summary that can stand alone and make the case
  • A rigorous market analysis: size, growth, segments and trends
  • An honest competitive assessment
  • A credible business model and unit economics
  • A team section — investors fund people as much as ideas
  • Financial projections built from defensible assumptions

Making financial projections credible

Build your projections bottom-up: how many customers can you realistically acquire in the first year, through which channels, at what conversion rate and average revenue? Every number should trace back to an assumption you can defend. For help pressure-testing them, contact your local Enterprise Europe Network office.

Present three scenarios — base case, upside and downside. The downside is often the most important: it shows you have stress-tested the model.

The competitive analysis that builds confidence

Stating that you have no competitors is one of the most reliable signals that a founder has not thought rigorously about their market. Name your competitors, assess their strengths honestly, and explain specifically where you win.
